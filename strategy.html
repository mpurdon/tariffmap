Game theory

A prisoner's dilemma, played in tariffs

In a trade war, everyone's a prisoner. Each government does better by taxing imports if its partner doesn't hit back, and every government does worse when they all do it. That is the textbook prisoner's dilemma, and the tariffs on the map are a long game of it. This page explains the dilemma, shows how each economy has actually played, and lets you run the strategies against each other.

1 · The dilemma

Two countries each choose: keep trade open, or impose tariffs. Pick a move for each and see what it's worth to them.

Points are illustrative welfare for A · B, the standard values for this game. Only their order matters: a free ride (5) beats mutual openness (3), which beats a trade war (1), which beats being the only one hit (0).

Why tariffs can pay at all: a large economy that taxes imports pushes some of the cost onto foreign exporters, who cut their prices to keep the market. Harry Johnson showed in 1953 that such an "optimum tariff" leaves the imposing country better off if nobody retaliates, and that retaliation can leave both worse off than free trade. Whatever B does, A scores more by imposing tariffs, and the same holds for B, so both impose them and land on 1 · 1, when 3 · 3 was there for the taking.

The way out is to repeat the game and make promises enforceable. Bagwell and Staiger read the GATT and the WTO as exactly that: a rulebook that lets large economies commit to the 3 · 3 cell together. A tariff war is what the game looks like when that commitment breaks down.

3 · Play it out

The dilemma bites hardest when it repeats. In 1980 Robert Axelrod ran tournaments where strategies played each other round after round. The winner was the simplest: tit-for-tat. Pick two strategies and watch a 40-round trade war. Then add mistakes (a measure misread as hostile, or imposed by accident) and watch what happens to strategies that never forgive.

A model, not a forecast. Real trade wars have more than two players, uneven stakes and elections; what transfers is the shape of the incentives.

4 · What the game teaches

  • Be nice. Strategies that never tariff first win on average. Opening a trade war wins one round and loses the rest.
  • Retaliate. Never retaliating invites exploitation. Canada's and China's same-week countermeasures are tit-for-tat in practice.
  • Forgive. With mistakes, strict tit-for-tat locks into an echo of alternating retaliation. Strategies that let some hits go unanswered recover; the EU's suspended countermeasures and the many negotiated caps are forgiveness in practice.
  • Be clear. Tit-for-tat works because it is easy to read. Announced, rule-based responses let the other side predict the cost of escalating.
